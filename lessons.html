Lesson 1

Animation Through Ages

Pre-Digital Optical Inventions & The Four Eras of Animation

The Four Eras of Animation

The evolution of animation is broadly categorized into three distinct eras:

  • 1. The Pre-Cinematic Era: (Before the 1900s) The terms refers to optical toys, shadow plays, and mechanical devices that created the illusion of movement using sequential images.
  • 2. The Traditional (Cel) Animation Era: (1900s - 1920s) This is where The Golden Age (1920s - 1960s) of hand-drawn animation occurred, where artists drew frame-by-frame on transparent celluloid sheets (cel).
  • 3. The Television Animation Era: (1960s - 1990s) As televisions became common in households, animation shifted from movie theaters to television screens.
  • 4. The Digital Animation Era: (1990s - Present) The modern era utilizing computers, 3D modeling, CGI, and digital software pipelines.

Examples of Pre-Digital Optical Inventions

Before the invention of motion picture cameras, inventors and artists created ingenious mechanical devices to trick the human eye into seeing continuous motion. Here are the primary optical inventions used during this era:

Figure 1.1: Thaumatrope (1824)

The Thaumatrope

A small disc with different pictures on each side (such as a bird and a cage). When spun quickly using attached strings, persistence of vision blends the two images into one.

Figure 1.2: Phenakistoscope (1832)

The Phenakistoscope

Invented by Joseph Plateau, this spinning cardboard disc featured sequential drawings and radial slits, creating a fluid moving sequence when viewed in a mirror.

Figure 1.3: Zoetrope (1834)

The Zoetrope

A cylindrical device featuring vertical slits with sequential drawings inside. When spun, multiple viewers could watch the animated sequence simultaneously.

The Flipbook

A book with a series of images that vary gradually from page to page. When pages are flipped rapidly, the sequential images simulate motion through persistence of vision.

Figure 1.5: Evolution of Early Animation Pioneers

The Pioneers of Cartoons

Emile Cohl is widely recognized as the Father of Animated Cartoons, transitioning optical toys into cinematic motion pictures.

Video 1.1: Local Video Discussion - Pre-Digital Animation

Featured Lesson Video

Watch the first ever animated video by Emile Cohl.

Quick Summary
  • Three Eras of Animation: Pre-Cinematic Era (optical toys), Traditional Era (hand-drawn cel animation), and Digital Era (computer & CGI).
  • Pre-Cinematic Era: Focused on mechanical optical illusions before the invention of film cameras.
  • Thaumatrope (1824): Two-sided spinning disc blending two images into one.
  • Phenakistoscope (1832): Spinning slotted disc viewed in a mirror for looping motion.
  • Zoetrope (1834): Cylindrical drum with slits allowing multiple viewers to see animations.
  • Flipbook (1868): Book of sequential images flipped rapidly to simulate motion.
  • Emile Cohl: Father of Animated Cartoons.
Lesson 2

Launch Your Animation Studio

Production Pipeline & Key Roles

Launching an Animation Studio

Launching an animation studio means creating a company that produces cartoons, anime, movies, or educational videos. To ensure success, understanding the core foundational steps and organizational workflow is essential.

Key Steps in Launching a Studio

  • 1. Determine the Purpose: Decide what type of animation to create and identify the target audience.
  • 2. Develop the Concept: Create story ideas, characters, and visual styles.
  • 3. Prepare Equipment and Software: Use computers, drawing tablets, and animation software to produce animations.
  • 4. Organize the Team: Assign dedicated roles to ensure smooth workflow and efficient production.
  • 5. Plan the Production: Prepare the budget, schedule, and resources to avoid delays.
  • 6. Create and Promote the Animation: Produce, edit, check, and release the animation to the target audience.

Studio Roles & Organization

Each team member plays a vital role in bringing an animated project from concept to final screen release:

  • Producer: Oversees the entire project, managing business operations and funding.
  • Production Manager: Manages budget, schedule, daily resources, and studio timelines.
  • Storyboard Artist: Draws important scenes and visualizes the script into sequential panels.
  • Character Designer: Designs the physical appearance, costumes, and expressions of characters.
  • Animator: Creates fluid movement and performance for the characters.
  • Editor: Arranges, times, and improves scenes during post-production.

Quick Summary
  • Studio Definition: Creating a company that produces cartoons, anime, movies, or educational videos.
  • Determine Purpose: Decide animation type and identify target audience.
  • Develop Concept: Create story ideas, characters, and visual styles.
  • Prepare Equipment: Utilize computers, drawing tablets, and animation software.
  • Team Roles: Producer (oversees project), Production Manager (budget/schedule), Storyboard Artist (draws scenes), Character Designer (character styling), Animator (movement), and Editor (arranges scenes).
  • Plan Production: Prepare budgets and resources to avoid delays.
  • Conclusion: A successful animation studio requires creativity, teamwork, proper planning, and effective management.
Lesson 3

7 Visual Elements & Typography

Foundational Building Blocks of Art and Design

The 7 Basic Elements of Visual Art

The 7 basic elements of visual art are the foundational building blocks artists use to create a composition.

Figure 3.1: Line Element

1. Line

A moving point across a surface. It can be straight, curved, thick, thin, horizontal, vertical, or diagonal to outline shapes and guide the eye.

Figure 3.2: Shape Element

2. Shape

A flat, two-dimensional enclosed area. Shapes can be geometric (like squares and circles) or organic (like natural, free-flowing forms).

Figure 3.3: Form Element

3. Form

A three-dimensional object that has length, width, and depth. While actual form exists in sculptures and ceramics, it can also be implied in 2D art using shading and perspective.

Figure 3.4: Space Element

4. Space

The area around, within, or between objects. It includes positive space (main subject) and negative space (background), plus the illusion of depth.

Figure 3.5: Color Element

5. Color

Formed from reflected light and defined by three properties: hue (color name), value (lightness/darkness), and intensity/chroma (brightness/dullness).

Figure 3.6: Value Element

6. Value

The relative lightness or darkness of a color or surface. Ranging from pure white to deep black, value creates contrast and builds volume.

Figure 3.7: Texture Element

7. Texture

The surface quality of an artwork. It can be actual (how a surface physically feels, like rough canvas or smooth clay) or implied (the visual illusion of a tactile surface rendered with paint or pencil).

Typography in Design

Typography is the visual art and arrangement of type to communicate both literally and visually. Beyond carrying words, typography functions as a core visual building block in graphic design.

Key typographical terms and text formatting styles include:

  • Font: A complete graphical set or family of text characters sharing a specific weight, width, and style (e.g., Arial, Helvetica).
  • Sans Serif / Sans: Typefaces that do not feature small projecting features called "serifs" at the end of strokes, offering a clean, modern aesthetic.
  • Italic: A stylized font variation where characters slant to the right, often used for emphasis or titles.
  • Bold: A heavier, thicker weight variant of a font used to draw immediate visual attention or highlight key terms.
  • Underlined: Text formatting featuring a horizontal line running directly beneath the baseline of the characters.

The three primary types of text spacing in typography are:

  • 1. Tracking (Letter-Spacing): The uniform adjustment of spacing across an entire word, phrase, or block of text.
  • 2. Kerning: The adjustment of space between two specific individual characters to achieve visual balance.
  • 3. Leading (Line-Spacing): The vertical baseline-to-baseline distance between consecutive lines of text.
Quick Summary
  • 7 Visual Elements: Line, Shape, Form, Space, Color, Value, and Texture.
  • Typography Styles: Font, Sans Serif / Sans, Italic, Bold, and Underlined.
  • Three Spacing Types: Tracking (letter-spacing), Kerning, and Leading (line-spacing).
Lesson 4

7 Principles of Design

Balance, Contrast, Movement & Composition

The 7 Principles of Design

The principles of design are guidelines on how to arrange and combine the visual elements (like line, shape, and color) to create aesthetically pleasing and effective compositions.

Figure 4.1: Balance Principle

1. Balance

The distribution of visual weight in a composition. It can be symmetrical (even on both sides), asymmetrical (balanced through contrasting elements), or radial (centered).

Figure 4.2: Contrast Principle

2. Contrast

The juxtaposition of different elements (such as light vs. dark colors, rough vs. smooth textures, or large vs. small shapes) to create visual interest and highlight key areas.

Figure 4.3: Movement Principle

3. Movement

The path the viewer’s eye takes through the artwork. Guided by lines, shapes, and colors, it directs attention across the composition.

Figure 4.4: Emphasis Principle

4. Emphasis (Focal Point)

The creation of a focal point that draws the viewer’s attention first, making a specific element stand out from the rest of the design.

Figure 4.5: Pattern Principle

5. Pattern

The repeating of a specific object, shape, color, or visual element consistently throughout a composition.

Figure 4.6: Rhythm Principle

6. Rhythm

A sense of organized movement or tempo created by the regular repetition or variation of design elements, establishing visual cadence.

Figure 4.7: Unity Principle

7. Unity and Harmony

The feeling of completeness and wholeness where all individual elements work together cohesively to support the overall design message.

Quick Summary
  • 7 Principles of Design: Balance (distribution of visual weight), Contrast (juxtaposition of differing elements), Movement (guiding the viewer's eye), Emphasis (focal point creation), Pattern (consistent repetition), Rhythm (visual tempo/cadence), and Unity/Harmony (cohesive wholeness).
Lesson 5

Image File Formats & Graphics

Raster vs. Vector & Standard Image Formats

Raster vs. Vector Graphics

Understanding the fundamental difference between raster and vector graphics is essential in digital animation and graphic design:

  • Raster: It is made up of a grid of tiny pixels. When you stretch or enlarge it, it becomes blurry or pixelated (you can see the individual dots).
  • Vector: It is made up of mathematical formulas, lines, and curves. No matter how much you scale it up or down, it never loses quality or becomes blurry.

Standard Image File Formats

Different image file formats serve specific purposes in asset creation and animation workflows. Here are the primary examples with dedicated visual containers:

Figure 5.1: JPEG Raster Asset

1. .JPEG (Joint Photographic Experts Group)

It is a standard image format for containing lossy and compressed image data, widely used for photographs.

Figure 5.2: HEIC Container Asset

2. .HEIC (High Efficiency File Format)

A modern file format traditionally used by Apple devices for high-quality images with smaller file sizes.

Figure 5.3: PNG Transparent Asset

3. .PNG (Portable Network Group)

A lossless image format prized for supporting a transparent background and sharp graphical details.

Figure 5.4: GIF Animated Asset

4. .GIF (Graphic Interchange Format)

An image format widely recognized because it supports short looping animation sequences.

Quick Summary
  • Raster Graphics: Made of pixel grids; becomes blurry or pixelated when stretched or enlarged.
  • Vector Graphics: Made of mathematical formulas and curves; never loses quality when scaled.
  • .JPEG: Joint Photographic Experts Group; standard format for compressed lossy images.
  • .HEIC: High Efficiency File Format; traditionally utilized by Apple devices.
  • .PNG: Portable Network Group; ideal for lossless compression and transparent backgrounds.
  • .GIF: Graphic Interchange Format; popular format that supports simple animations.
Lesson 6

Camera Shots, Angles & Movements

Cinematic Framing, Storyboards, and Visual Storytelling

Introduction to Cinematic Framing

Storyboard: A visual representation of a film sequence that breaks down the action into individual panels.

Framing Height: A guide for accurate cinematography and photography. A camera shot is considered the basic unit of film language.

1. Camera Shots According to Size

Framing sizes determine how much of the subject and environment are visible in the frame.

Figure 6.1: Close-up Shot Example

Close-up Shot

Shows the actor from their shoulder up to their head, emphasizing facial expressions and emotion.

Figure 6.2: Medium Shot Example

Medium Shot

A type of camera shot that shows the subject from the waist up, balancing character interaction and environment.

Figure 6.3: Full Shot Example

Full Shot

Reveals where your actor is positioned and displays the entire body of the character.

Figure 6.4: Long Shot Example

Long Shot

It is similar to the full shot but covers a much wider area, emphasizing the subject's environment.

2. Camera Shots According to Position on the Screen

These shots define how subjects and scenes are structured in relation to context and narrative perspective.

Figure 6.5: Master Shot Example

Master Shot

Keeps all the characters in view and shows the action of a scene from start to finish.

Figure 6.6: Establishing Shot Example

Establishing Shot

A scene or shot that provides a clear overview of the setting and location.

Figure 6.7: POV Shot Example

Point of View (POV) Shot

A subjective shot that shows the scene precisely the way a character sees it.

Figure 6.8: Reaction Shot Example

Reaction Shot

A camera shot focusing on a character's emotional reaction to something or someone.

Figure 6.9: Over the Shoulder Shot Example

Over the Shoulder Shot

Used to facilitate conversation and dialogue interaction between two persons.

3. Camera Shots According to Movement

Dynamic motion helps follow action and adds cinematic energy to scenes.

Figure 6.10: Tracking

Tracking Shot

Also known as a “Traveling Shot.” A shot in which the camera moves alongside the action in a scene.

Figure 6.11: Panning

Panning Shot

The horizontal (Left to Right) movement of a shot taken from a fixed camera location.

Figure 6.12: Tilting

Tilting Shot

The vertical (Up and Down) movement of a shot taken from a fixed camera location.

4. Camera Shots According to Angle

Camera angles change the psychological perception of a character's power, status, or vulnerability.

Figure 6.13: High Angle Example

High Angle Shot

Shot taken from above the subject with the camera directed downward, making the subject look smaller or vulnerable.

Figure 6.14: Low Angle Example

Low Angle Shot

The camera is placed below eye level, near the ground, and aimed upwards to capture the subject with power.

Figure 6.15: Bird's Eye View Example

Bird’s Eye View

A high angle shot taken to the extreme, as when shooting a street or layout directly from a top building.

Figure 6.16: Worm's Eye View Example

Worm’s Eye View

Usually done by setting the camera down directly to the floor level looking straight up.

Figure 6.17: Canted View Example

Canted View (Dutch Angle)

If you want to add flavor or tension to your static shot, you can slant your camera to produce a canted shot.

Quick Summary
  • Storyboard & Framing: Visual breakdown of a film sequence; framing height serves as a guide for cinematography.
  • Shot Sizes: Close-up (shoulders up), Medium (waist up), Full (entire body/position), and Long Shot (wider area context).
  • Screen Position Shots: Master Shot (all characters/full action), Establishing Shot (overview of setting), POV Shot (character's perspective), Reaction Shot (emotional response), and Over-the-Shoulder (dialogue interaction).
  • Camera Movements: Tracking (moving with action), Panning (horizontal fixed movement), and Tilting (vertical fixed movement).
  • Camera Angles: High Angle (looking down), Low Angle (looking up), Bird’s Eye View (extreme overhead), Worm’s Eye View (ground level), and Canted View (slanted/Dutch angle for tension).
